Why this works: the garden of forking paths

With one pre-specified test, a useless drug "wins" 5% of the time. Test 20 independent outcomes and the chance of at least one false positive is 1 − 0.9520 ≈ 64%. Add subgroups and an optional stopping rule and it climbs further. Then publish only the winner, call it the "primary outcome", and leave out the 19 losers. Nobody can tell, unless the trial was registered in advance. The classic demonstration is the huge ISIS-2 heart-attack trial: split by star sign, aspirin appeared useless, even slightly harmful, for Geminis and Libras. The authors did that deliberately, to show how absurd subgroup fishing is.

What the research-on-research says

Same data, opposite conclusion

GSK's Study 329: Paxil in teenagers

Published 2001 under academic authors’ names; first drafted by a company-paid writer
“Paroxetine is generally well tolerated and effective for major depression in adolescents.”
Independent reanalysis of the raw data, BMJ 2015
“Neither paroxetine nor high dose imipramine showed efficacy for major depression in adolescents, and there was an increase in harms with both drugs.”

The original paper reported outcomes that were not in the trial protocol, mixed up primary and secondary outcomes, and classed suicidal events as "emotional lability". GSK’s $3 billion guilty plea in 2012 included promoting Paxil for under-18s. When the reanalysis appeared in 2015, the journal still declined to retract the 2001 paper. Le Noury et al., BMJ 2015.

Meanwhile, in Australia

It didn't all happen in America

A fake journal, and a "hit list", exposed in Melbourne

The Federal Court Vioxx class action (2009) revealed that Merck had paid Elsevier to publish the Australasian Journal of Bone & Joint Medicine. It looked like a peer-reviewed journal but was sponsored marketing. Internal emails read out in court listed doctors to "neutralise" or "discredit".

The largest product-liability settlement in Australian history

In 2019 the Federal Court found J&J’s Ethicon negligent over pelvic mesh implants and found it had breached consumer law. After failed appeals, it settled in 2022 for A$300 million, covering more than 11,000 women.

Tasmania’s poppies and America’s opioid crisis

J&J’s former subsidiary Tasmanian Alkaloids developed a high-thebaine poppy that became a key raw material for oxycodone. That link featured in the Oklahoma case against J&J. Australia’s PBS, which negotiates prices centrally, is a big reason most medicines here cost a fraction of US prices.

05The verdict

Two questions: are they good or bad? And are they getting any better? Here is the evidence on both sides, followed by a straight answer.

The case for the defence

  • They make the medicines. Statins, insulin analogues, HIV antiretrovirals (a diagnosis that once meant death now comes with a near-normal life expectancy), hepatitis C cures, cancer immunotherapy, GLP-1 drugs. Almost all were developed or brought to market by these companies.
  • COVID-19 vaccines are estimated to have prevented around 14.4 million deaths in their first year alone.
  • Real philanthropy exists: Merck’s ivermectin donations for river blindness since 1987, Novartis’s not-for-profit malaria drug, GSK’s malaria vaccine, J&J’s TB drug.
  • Some transparency is better now. In the EU, commercial sponsors post trial results far more reliably than universities and hospitals do (68% v 11% in 2018). GSK, J&J and others now share trial data with outside researchers.
  • Most employees are not villains. Many of the biggest cases were exposed by the companies’ own staff acting as whistleblowers.

The case for the prosecution

  • Every one of the twelve has paid government penalties for misconduct since 1996, and all twelve have had a company entity plead guilty to a crime or accept a deferred prosecution.
  • Repeat offending is the norm. Pfizer, J&J, GSK and Novartis each have multiple guilty pleas or kickback cases, some committed while already on "corporate integrity" probation.
  • Crime pays. Record fines amount to days or weeks of revenue, while the drugs involved earned billions. Settlements usually come without admitting liability.
  • People were harmed. Vioxx heart attacks, suicidal teenagers on Paxil, nursing-home patients sedated with off-label antipsychotics, and an opioid epidemic that has killed hundreds of thousands of Americans.
  • Almost nobody goes to jail. The Insys founder and Purdue’s 2007 executives (probation) are rare exceptions. Corporations plead guilty; executives keep their bonuses.

Are they getting better? A scorecard

The bottom line

Not evil. Not good. Rational, in a system where cheating usually pays.

The evidence does not support "a few bad apples". Across all twelve companies, several decades and dozens of countries, the same tactics recur, because they are profitable and the expected penalty is smaller than the expected gain. These companies are neither charities nor cartoon villains. They are profit-maximisers that respond to incentives, and for thirty years the incentives have rewarded bending the rules.

Are they getting better? Partly, on the science. Trial registration, results databases, data-sharing and payment disclosure laws have made the crudest ways of burying data much harder, and big pharma now reports trial results more reliably than academia does. Not on the money. Kickbacks dressed up as speaker programmes and charity donations kept turning up through 2026. US launch prices hit records, patent thickets got thicker, and lobbying spend hit new highs. The fall in criminal mega-settlements since 2013 partly reflects narrower prosecutions, so it is weak evidence of cleaner behaviour.

What actually changes behaviour has been forced transparency, whistleblower rewards, and, rarely, personal liability for executives. Where those exist, things improve. Where they don't, they don't.

07Method & sources

What is counted. The headline totals include only money paid to governments (criminal fines, forfeitures and civil settlements or judgments) that is final. Private lawsuits (product liability, class actions, shareholder suits) are tracked separately and shown only where labelled. Tax disputes are never counted as penalties. Rulings that were overturned or are still under appeal are listed but excluded.

Settling is not admitting. Many civil settlements were made without any admission of liability, and the companies denied the allegations. Where a company entity pleaded guilty, was convicted, or accepted a deferred prosecution agreement, the case is marked. Descriptions follow the language of the official announcements and court findings.

Money. Amounts are nominal US dollars as announced, not adjusted for inflation. Non-US fines are converted at approximately the exchange rate of the year of the decision. A case is attributed to today’s parent company if the parent later acquired the business (for example, Allergan’s cases are under AbbVie, and Abbott’s pre-2013 drug cases are also under AbbVie because AbbVie inherited that business). "Revenue" is the 2025 figure for the whole group, converted to US$ where needed.

Not exhaustive. This is a curated set of about 120 of the most significant cases, not every penalty ever paid. Public Citizen counted 482 US government settlements with drug makers totalling $62.3 billion between 1991 and 2021. Smaller cases, most state-level settlements and most private litigation are not included, so the real totals are higher.
